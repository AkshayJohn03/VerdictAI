=== SCENE 1 — Who grades the grader? ===
Companies today use one A I model to grade the answers of another: rate this reply, which answer is better? That grader is called a judge. But here is the awkward question nobody asks: who grades the grader? The judge is itself a machine, with habits. It may prefer whichever answer it read first. It may favor longer answers, mistaking length for substance. And it may be kinder to writing that sounds like its own. Verdict A I is the quality-control lab for that grader: an exam grader that first learns from human teachers.

=== SCENE 2 — The core idea: a quality-control lab ===
The core idea in one sentence: treat the judge as a measured component, like any other part of the system. Think of training a new teaching assistant. First, hand it a marking scheme, and it grades with that scheme. Then compare its marks against experienced teachers, and measure how closely it agrees. Then give the students a proper exam, one that could not have leaked. Finally, put an alarm on the door that blocks a release when scores genuinely drop. Four stations: grade, check, build the exam, gate the release.

=== SCENE 3 — Station one: the rubric ===
Station one: the rubric. A rubric is a written marking scheme. Instead of one vague score out of ten, the judge grades each dimension separately: accuracy out of five, evidence out of five, tone out of five. Each level has an anchor, a plain description of what that score means, so a four means the same thing every time. And each verdict must quote its evidence: the exact words from the answer that earned the score. Breaking one big opinion into small graded parts is rubric decomposition, and it is far less noisy.

=== SCENE 4 — Pairwise judging, both orders ===
Some questions are comparisons: which answer is better, A or B? That is pairwise judging, and models compare more reliably than they score absolutely. But watch the order. Verdict A I asks the judge twice: A against B, then B against A. If the verdict flips when the order swaps, that is position bias, a reading habit, not a judgement. Flips are counted as the position bias rate, and a flipped item scores zero point five, a tie, instead of a wrong answer. Two more habits are measured directly. Verbosity bias: does the score climb with length? That slope gets a statistical test. Self-preference: does the judge favor writing from its own model family? Those items get flagged.

=== SCENE 5 — Kappa: agreement beyond luck ===
Now, how do we know the judge grades like a human? Humans grade a sample too. You could count exact agreement, but that number lies: two graders flipping coins agree a quarter of the time by pure luck. Cohen's kappa measures agreement beyond luck: observed agreement minus chance agreement, divided by one minus chance agreement. Zero means a coin flip, one means perfect. The worked example, pinned by a test: the judge grades zero, zero, one, one; the human grades zero, one, one, one. Observed agreement, three quarters. Chance agreement, one half. Kappa: zero point five. Moderate agreement. For zero-to-five scales, weighted kappa gives partial credit, and Spearman checks the ranking.

=== SCENE 6 — Curving the grades: isotonic regression + PAV ===
The judge says five; the humans say three point five. The judge is not evil, it is miscalibrated. It needs its grades curved, like a teacher curving an exam. Isotonic regression draws the best mapping from judge scores to human scores under one rule: higher must never become lower. No curve shape is assumed, so it captures saturation at the top and dead zones at the bottom. The algorithm is pool-adjacent-violators: walk the data left to right; whenever a block dips below its neighbor, pool them and average until the staircase only climbs. The report prints agreement before and after, so overfitting on small samples shows itself. Platt scaling, the alternative, fits one rigid sigmoid, and real judge error is messier.

=== SCENE 7 — A better exam: golden datasets + decontamination ===
Next, the exam itself. A golden dataset is a fixed, versioned set of test questions with known-good answers. The generator builds variety on purpose: topics crossed with personas crossed with difficulty, plus nasty edge cases like hidden instructions. A coverage matrix marks every cell of that grid, and a test asserts nothing is left empty. The dataset ships like code: a JSON-L file, a manifest of S H A 256 hashes, a changelog. Then, decontamination: if any exam question shares eight consecutive words with the corpus the model may have memorised, it is dropped. Eight words in a row is a quote, not a coincidence. Don't let the exam leak into the textbook.

=== SCENE 8 — The alarm: bootstrap CI + the gate ===
Now the alarm. Every scoring run is snapshotted: an append-only record with the model version, the dataset hash, and every per-item score. Nothing is ever edited. When a new version arrives, Verdict A I pairs the runs item by item: baseline score minus candidate score, per question. Is the drop real, or luck? The bootstrap resamples those paired deltas two thousand times, and reports where the average drop lives ninety-five percent of the time. If that confidence interval excludes zero, luck is unlikely. A Wilcoxon test backs it up on small samples. And because huge datasets make tiny shifts look significant, the gate also demands a minimum effect size, Cliff's delta. Only then: regression. Exit code one. The build fails, a webhook alert fires. Noise passes in silence.

=== SCENE 9 — The numbers ===
So, does it work? Measured, not claimed. One hundred seventy-seven automated tests pass offline in under two seconds, no API key, no network. The kappa example you saw is pinned at exactly zero point five. Quadratic weighted kappa, at two sevenths. And the gate is proven against a planted crime: a replay adapter pretends to be a model having a bad day, replaying golden answers degraded to seventy-five percent quality. The mean score falls from five point zero to four point zero seven: a nineteen percent drop. The confidence interval sits far above zero, the effect size is maximal, and the gate exits one. The noise run, grown from the same distribution, passes in silence.

=== SCENE 10 — The lab has a front door ===
The lab also has a front door. The same harness runs behind FastAPI: submit an eval run, get an I-D back immediately, poll until it succeeds. A webhook can call your systems when the run completes, signed with an HMAC, so a fake all-clear cannot be injected. API keys are stored only as S H A 256 hashes, never in plain text. And every request may carry a correlation I-D, echoed on every response, so one run's story stays grep-able across systems.

=== SCENE 11 — The 30-second recap ===
Thirty-second recap. The judge grades with a written rubric, not vibes: anchored criteria, quoted evidence. Comparisons run in both orders, so position bias is caught and counted, along with verbosity and self-preference. Kappa measures agreement with humans beyond luck; our worked example is zero point five. Isotonic regression curves judge scores onto the human scale, computed by hand with PAV. Golden datasets are generated on a coverage grid, decontaminated against the corpus, and versioned with hashes. And the gate separates real regressions from noise, blocking the build only for the real ones. That is Verdict A I: nothing is trusted without a number.
